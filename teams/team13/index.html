Data Speaks!!

What can 100 years of Spotify data tell us about music? We blend one song into another, trace how music's sound changed, and sort songs into "tribes" by sound alone.

Problem statement

Every Spotify track comes with 12 numbers that describe how it sounds, such as danceability, energy, acousticness and tempo. We wanted to see what those numbers can tell us, so we asked three questions:

  • DJ Transition Engine: can we get from one song to a very different one through a smooth chain of real songs, like a DJ set?
  • Sound Timeline: did music really change over the last 100 years the way people assume, and how sure can we be?
  • Music Tribes Explorer: what natural groups appear if we sort songs only by how they sound, ignoring genre and artist?

Dataset

We used the Spotify Dataset 1921-2020 from Kaggle (by Yamaç Eren Ay). After cleaning, it has 523,585 tracks, each with 12 audio features plus artist, genre, popularity and release year.

The DJ engine and the tribes use a 100,001-song sample, spread evenly across decades, to keep things fast. The Sound Timeline uses every track. Keep in mind that features like acousticness come from Spotify's own models, not direct measurements, and the data only covers what is on Spotify today.

Approach

We cleaned the data once and shared it across all three parts. Then each part took its own route:

  • DJ Transition Engine: link every song to its 14 most similar-sounding songs, then use Dijkstra's shortest-path algorithm to find the smoothest chain between any two.
  • Sound Timeline: follow five features (acousticness, speechiness, liveness, instrumentalness and tempo) across the century with one statistical test each, then check whether they changed together. Along the way we found and removed about 22,000 tracks that were not music.
  • Music Tribes Explorer: group songs into six "tribes" with K-Means clustering, check them against genres and decades, and draw them as a 2-D map with UMAP.

Tools used

Python and Jupyter notebooks, with pandas, NumPy, SciPy, scikit-learn, statsmodels, umap-learn and matplotlib.
