Results

What our three modules found, one graph at a time.

DJ Transition Engine

The DJ Transition Engine connects two very different songs with a chain of real songs, each one a small step from the last, the way a DJ builds a set. We linked each of the 100,001 songs to its 14 most similar-sounding neighbours, which forms one connected network, and used Dijkstra's shortest-path algorithm to find the smoothest route between any two songs.

Adele's Someone Like You reaches Avicii's Wake Me Up in 9 steps, The Beatles' Hello, Goodbye (1967) reaches Dua Lipa's Levitating (2020) in 13, and two Ed Sheeran songs need only 3. We saved 50 ready-made transitions for the website.

How long is a typical route?

We picked 1,000 random pairs of songs and found the route between each one. Every pair could be connected. Most routes take 6 to 12 steps (median 9, longest 19), so even very different songs are only about ten songs apart. The total distance travelled (right) has two humps, a smaller one around 5 and a taller one around 15.

Which features change along a route?

No single feature does all the work. Track length, tempo, danceability, loudness, valence, energy and acousticness all change by similar amounts along a route. Mode, time signature, instrumentalness and speechiness change much less, mostly because most songs have nearly the same values for them.

Sound Timeline: one feature at a time, 1922-2021

The Sound Timeline follows one audio feature at a time across 100 years of music, using all 523,585 tracks, and gives each feature its own statistical test. Along the way we found that 22,317 tracks (4.26%) were not music at all. They were audiobooks, German radio plays (Hörspiel) and other spoken recordings, so we built a rule to remove them.

Acousticness over a century

Until about 1950 almost every song was acoustic, with an average around 0.9. It then dropped sharply from the mid-1950s to around 1980 and kept sliding slowly to about 0.3. The shaded band also gets much wider after the 1960s, so modern music is a real mix of acoustic and electronic songs.

Liveness barely changes

Liveness stays almost flat for the whole century, around 0.21 to 0.23, with only a small dip after 2010. Most songs sound like studio recordings, and a smaller number of truly live tracks pull the average up. With no trend to test, we compared live and studio versions of the same songs instead (Figures 15 to 17).

Instrumental music fades after 1960

Before the mid-1950s, instrumental tracks and songs with vocals sat side by side. After about 1960 that changes fast, and by 1970 the vast majority of songs have vocals. Because most songs score close to zero here, the average is misleading, so later we count the share of instrumental songs instead.

Speechiness before cleaning

The raw data shows huge spikes before 1937, a bump in the 1980s and a rise after 2010. The first two turned out to be fake. The early spikes are audiobooks dated by when the book was written, and the 1980s bump is German radio drama, which made up over 10% of that decade. Only the rise after 2010 is real.

Tempo over a century

Average tempo is lowest in the mid-1940s, at about 106 BPM. It climbs to about 120 BPM by 1980 and stays there. This chart still includes non-music tracks. Later we look at how spread out tempos are in each decade, not just the average.

Acousticness tipped in 1969

We fitted an S-shaped curve to the yearly averages. Its midpoint, where the change was fastest, is 1969. The whole shift took about a generation, from roughly 1952 to 1986, as acousticness fell from 0.90 to 0.32, and the curve explains 97% of the variation. Electric guitars, synths and studio production are likely reasons, but we did not test them.

How well does the curve fit?

The bottom panel shows what the curve misses each year. With a perfect fit these misses would be random, but they come in long waves. The real change was lopsided: it held on through the 1950s, dropped very sharply in the late 1960s, and is still slowly falling today, below where the curve says it should level off.

How sure are we about 1969?

We resampled the songs 2,000 times and refitted the curve each time. The midpoint always landed between 1969.0 and 1969.5. That shows a different sample of songs would not change the answer, but it does not prove the S-shape is right, so "the late 1960s" is the safer claim.

Removing non-music tracks

Orange is speechiness before cleaning and blue is after. We found the non-music tracks in three steps: titles like "Chapter 12", artists who are almost always speaking, and 19 genre tags such as hoerspiel, reading, comedy and poetry. Removing them wipes out the early spikes and the 1980s bump. From 2000 on the two lines almost overlap.

Speechiness after cleaning

With the non-music gone, speechiness stays flat at about 0.06 to 0.09 for around 90 years. The one real change is a rise from 0.077 in 2010 to 0.132 in 2021. That fits with rap and hip-hop going mainstream, but we did not test genre, so we cannot say it is the cause.

Three kinds of songs, not two

Speechiness is not a simple sung-or-spoken split. It falls into three groups: mostly sung (62% of songs), a bit talky (26%) and speech-heavy (12%). The speech-heavy group is very spread out, which is why its curve lies almost flat along the bottom.

Did speechiness really shift?

Comparing 1960-2009 with 2012-2021, the newer curve sits to the right almost everywhere, so modern songs are talkier. The biggest gap between the curves is D = 0.196, a moderate and real shift. Average speechiness rose from 0.068 to 0.099, about 46%.

Live versus studio versions

We matched 2,697 songs that have both a studio and a live version by the same artist, and each dot is one song. The live version has more energy in 72% of pairs but is more popular in only 15%. On average it is about 16 points less popular, out of 100.

Is it because live versions come out later?

If live versions were less popular just because they had less time to collect streams, the gap would grow with the time between releases. It does not (r = 0.018). Pairs released within two years show about the same gap as pairs ten years apart. Our best guess is that the studio version is simply the one people stream by default, though the data cannot prove that.

How big are the differences?

The popularity drop is shared by most songs, mostly between −40 and 0, and is not caused by a few extreme cases. The energy boost is real but small for most songs, usually less than 0.3.

Instrumental songs: same story at any cutoff

Since most songs score near zero, we count the share of songs above a cutoff each year, trying 0.2, 0.5 and 0.7. The lines sit at different heights but have the same shape, and the trend test finds a decline at all three. The cutoff changes the numbers, not the conclusion.

A decline that reversed

The share of instrumental songs peaked at 49% in 1946, fell to 5% in 2003 and recovered to 18% by 2021. The trend test says the overall direction is down, but the straight orange line fits badly and even drops below zero, which is impossible for a share. The real story is a long fall followed by a partial comeback.

How varied were tempos in each decade?

Here we measure how spread out tempos are within each decade, not whether songs got faster. The 1940s had the widest mix (47 BPM) and the 1920s the narrowest (33 BPM), and Levene's test confirms the decades really differ. Before cleaning, the 1960s looked narrowest, because over a quarter of the 1920s data was not music.

Correlations that disappear

On raw yearly values (left), some features look strongly linked, such as acousticness and tempo at −0.85. But any two things that drift over 100 years will look linked. When we compare year-to-year changes instead (right), most links vanish, and acousticness with tempo drops to −0.04.

One big shift, or several?

If one cultural change drove everything, a single component would explain most of the variation. On raw values it explains 59%, but on year-to-year changes only 33%, and it takes three components to reach 80%. So the features mostly changed on their own, and tempo moves almost independently of the rest.

Does one feature lead another?

We tested whether changes in one feature help predict changes in another a few years later. If one truly led, a single bar would stand out. Neither pair shows that, and the bars flip between positive and negative. With only about 100 years of data, there is no clear leader.

Every turning point on one timeline

Each dot is a turning point found earlier: acousticness in 1969, instrumentalness in 1946, 2003 and 2007, speechiness around 2010, and tempo's narrowest and widest decades. Liveness has none. The dots do not line up, which is the clearest sign these were separate stories, not one big shift.

The century at a glance

All five cleaned trends on one time axis, with each turning point marked. It sums up the earlier results rather than adding a new one. The small speechiness spike in 1936 is a known leftover: 220 narrated tracks share their artist name with a 1960s rock band, so they slipped past the cleaning rule.

Tempo, decade by decade

Each curve shows how tempos were spread in one decade, so compare widths, not heights. The 1920s have one narrow peak near 115 BPM and the 1940s are the widest. The 2010s and 2020s show two clear humps, around 95 and 125 to 130 BPM.

Music gets talkier after 2015

This shows each year's mix of sung, a-bit-talky and speech-heavy songs. From the 1960s to about 1990, sung songs make up roughly 64 to 74% of each year. After about 2015 that falls to about 35%, while speech-heavy songs grow to about a third.

Decade fingerprints

Each decade gets a small radar chart across the five features. Early decades reach toward acousticness, liveness and instrumentalness. From the 1970s the shapes shrink toward tempo, and the 2020s is the only decade that spikes on speechiness. These are for a quick visual comparison only, since radar charts can exaggerate differences.

Music Tribes Explorer

The Music Tribes Explorer groups songs purely by how they sound. Genre, artist, year and popularity were never used. K-Means clustering on 100,001 songs gave six "tribes", which we then checked against genres, followed across decades and drew as a 2-D map. The tribes overlap quite a bit, so think of them as regions of one big continuum rather than separate islands.

How many tribes?

We tried 2 to 15 groups. Three groups only separate acoustic, energetic and instrumental music, which is too coarse. From 8 groups on, tiny groups appear that come from data quirks. Six is the elbow of the curve and the largest number with no junk group, so we chose six.

What makes each tribe different

Red means above average and blue means below. Most tribes stand out on one or two features: Spoken Word on speechiness, Live on Stage on liveness, Instrumental Meditations on instrumentalness, and Acoustic Storytellers on high acousticness and low energy. Sunlit and Midnight Anthems are almost identical except for key, since one is all major and the other all minor.

Each tribe's sound

Sunlit Anthems is the biggest tribe (33.9% of songs), then Acoustic Storytellers (24.5%) and Midnight Anthems (21.7%). The smaller ones are Instrumental Meditations (8.6%), Live on Stage (6.5%) and Spoken Word & Audio Drama (4.8%). Sunlit and Midnight Anthems look the same here because key, the one thing that separates them, is not on the chart.

A century of tribes

Acoustic Storytellers made up nearly half of the 1950s (48.6%) but only 9.4% of the 2020s, and Instrumental Meditations peaked in the 1940s. Meanwhile the two Anthem tribes grew from 12% of the 1920s to 77% of the 2020s, with the minor-key Midnight Anthems growing fastest, from 4% to 38%.

Two ways to draw the map

PCA (left) squashes everything into one overlapping cloud. UMAP (right) keeps similar songs close together much better (trustworthiness 0.94 against 0.78), so we use it for the website map. On UMAP only nearby points are meaningful, and distances between far-apart islands are not.

Do tribes match genres?

Genres were used only afterwards, as a check. Only Spoken Word & Audio Drama matches a single genre (85% spoken word). Every other tribe is a mix: Instrumental Meditations, for example, is 36% classical and 28% jazz. So the audio features capture how music is produced and played, not its genre.

What if we drop key and time signature?

Mode (major or minor) and time signature are on/off-style features, yet they count as much as energy. Rerunning with only the 10 continuous features gives cleaner groups (silhouette 0.187 against 0.161), and six groups still works best. Without mode, the major/minor pair is replaced by a split between fast, loud tracks and slower, happier danceable ones.

The map with and without key

With all 12 features (left), the map splits into two mirror-image "continents", one major-key and one minor-key, each with a copy of every tribe. Without mode (right), the two Anthem tribes merge into one cloud. We kept the 12-feature version as the main result because it matches the project plan and the DJ engine, but the 10-feature map is easier to read.

Anomalies, observations and patterns

  • Observation: All 100,001 songs form one connected network, so any two songs can be linked. Across 1,000 random pairs, a route takes about 9 steps on average and never more than 19.
  • Pattern: No single feature drives a DJ transition. Track length, tempo, danceability, loudness and others change by similar amounts, while mode, time signature, instrumentalness and speechiness barely move.
  • Anomaly: About 4% of the "music" (22,317 tracks) was really audiobooks, German radio plays and comedy. Removing it erased most of the apparent rise in speechiness and changed which decade had the narrowest tempo range. The Music Tribes Explorer found the same content from sound alone.
  • Observation: Speechiness stayed flat for about 90 years, then rose from 2010. This fits with rap and hip-hop going mainstream, but we did not test that directly.
  • Observation: Live versions have more energy in 72% of cases but are more popular in only 15%, and later release dates do not explain the gap.
  • Pattern: Each feature changed on its own schedule. Acousticness tipped around 1969, instrumental music peaked in 1946, bottomed out in 2003 and is recovering, and liveness never really changed. These are separate stories, not one shared shift.
  • Pattern: The tribes shift over time. Acoustic Storytellers fall from 48.6% of the 1950s to 9.4% of the 2020s, while the two Anthem tribes rise from 12% to 77%.
  • Observation: Sound alone does not rediscover genre. Only the Spoken Word tribe matches one genre, so the features capture production and instrumentation rather than genre.
  • Anomaly: Sunlit and Midnight Anthems are really one sound split by major versus minor key. Dropping key gives cleaner tribes, but we kept the 12-feature version to match the project plan and the DJ engine.
